03 / Chapter 8 · pp. 140–141

Meet the porcelain furnace

Think of it as a controlled heat-and-pressure environment, not simply a hot box.

Furnace ≠ sintering recipe

Basic parts and their roles

Schematic, not a model-specific diagram. The controller manages the firing programme; the thermocouple senses temperature; a pump provides reduced pressure when prescribed.

Book: ch. 8, pp. 140–141 (PDF pp. 149–150).

Types & programme controls

By control

Manual, automatic and programmable. The book identifies programmable units as the industry standard.

By how the work enters

  • Front-loading: work enters a horizontal chamber.
  • Vertical-loading: a platform carrying the work rises into the chamber.
  • Clamshell: the chamber closes over stationary work.

Settings to recognise

  • Entry / pre-dry temperature and drying time.
  • Heating rate, final temperature and hold time.
  • Vacuum start/end or pressure as directed.
  • Cooling or long-term cooling where required.
No universal cycle: parameters depend on the porcelain, alloy and furnace. Select the exact product’s instructions; check furnace calibration and the lab SOP.

Book: ch. 8, pp. 140–141, 154–155 · PDF pp. 149–150, 163–164.

Firing: understand the stages

DryRemove free liquid gradually; sudden heat can create steam and disturb the buildup.
HeatFollow the specified temperature rise and placement protocol.
Vacuum & fireUse the prescribed vacuum and hold; reduced pressure helps limit porosity, but does not guarantee zero pores.
Cool & checkFollow the system’s cooling schedule, then check contour and maturation.

04 / Core theory · pp. 141–143

Condensation, voids & shrinkage

Condensation makes the unfired buildup more closely packed while helping remove free liquid and reduce entrapped air.

Five methods in Naylor’s classification

VibrationGentle movement brings liquid to the surface.

Book: pp. 142–143 · PDF pp. 151–152.

How vibration and blotting work together

Wet buildup→Gentle vibration→Liquid to surface→Blot→Repeat as appropriate

Vibration helps free liquid move outward; blotting removes accessible surface liquid by capillary action. The early buildup is delicate: too much movement can shift material, mix layers or damage the contour.

Book: pp. 142–143 · PDF pp. 151–152.

Voids and firing shrinkage

  • Voids can weaken porcelain and scatter light, making it appear cloudier and less translucent.
  • During firing, particles bond and the buildup densifies. Naylor says porcelain shrinks towards its bulk.
  • The book describes 10–15% overbuilding as an approximation for its illustrated buildup, not a universal numerical instruction.

Book: p. 142 · PDF p. 151.

Voids: weak points + cloudy appearance

Voids can weaken porcelain and act as sites for crack initiation. They can scatter light, making a restoration look cloudier or less translucent. Sources include trapped air and processing defects.

Book: ch. 8, p. 142 (PDF p. 151). Diagram is illustrative.

Shrinkage: why the buildup changes

During firing and sintering, particles bond and the mass densifies as porosity decreases. The book notes that porcelain tends to shrink towards its bulk.

The book describes a 10–15% overbuild as an approximation for its buildup technique. It is not a universal rule for every system; do not assume the figure describes a specific dimensional measure.

Book: ch. 8, p. 142 (PDF p. 151). Diagram is not to scale.

05 / Opaquing · pp. 143–148

From metal to a masked surface

Opaque porcelain contributes to attachment, masks the metal and begins shade development.

Before & during application

  1. Verify readiness: the metal is finished, cleaned and conditioned or oxidised according to the alloy system; protect it from contamination.
  2. Prepare opaque: use the prescribed shade, powder or paste, and compatible liquid. Naylor describes glass rod, brush and spray methods.
  3. Apply: cover the porcelain-bearing surface evenly without pooling, following the material’s instructions.
  4. Dry & fire: clean unwanted material from non-veneered areas and intaglio, then use the product’s opaque cycle.
  5. Inspect: confirm coverage and recheck seating without forcing the coping onto the die. Additional applications may be needed under the system’s protocol.

Book: pp. 143–147 · PDF pp. 152–156.

Opaque is not a thick body buildup

The book distinguishes the relatively thin opaque application from body porcelain: indiscriminate blotting can lift or disturb it. In its illustrated method, excess liquid may be drawn gently from an edge when needed.

Keep the distinction: an example technique from the textbook is not a universal procedure for every paste or ceramic. Follow the exact material instructions, particularly for drying and firing.

Mixing and cross-contamination

Use clean, separate mixes; do not transfer opaque residue into body porcelain. Follow the product’s approved modelling liquid and re-wetting guidance.

Book: pp. 145–148 · PDF pp. 154–157.

06 / Body porcelain · pp. 147–156

Build, condense and fire

The main example is an anterior single crown with a metal lingual surface.

Standard anterior buildup

1 · MixPrepare separate dentin and enamel mixes. Too wet can slump; too dry can trap air between additions.
2 · DentinBuild the anatomical form and allow for shrinkage as appropriate to the case and material.
3 · Cut backRemove selected dentin to make room for enamel in the desired areas.
4 · EnamelAdd and blend enamel and any effect materials to achieve the intended appearance.
5 · CondenseUse an appropriate gentle method and remove free liquid without disturbing form.
6 · CheckInspect all aspects; clean intaglio and exposed metal surfaces of unwanted porcelain.
7 · First body bakeSupport the work on a tray and follow the specific drying, firing and cooling schedule.
8 · EvaluateInspect maturation, contours, contacts and shade; plan corrections as needed.

Book: pp. 147–156 · PDF pp. 156–165.

Moisture: too wet vs too dry

  • Too wet: difficult to keep the desired shape; buildup can slump or shift.
  • Too dry: additions may be harder to blend and air pockets may form between them.
  • Completely dried mix: the book cautions against simply re-wetting it; manage moisture before it dries and use the manufacturer’s current instructions.
  • Modelling liquid: can provide a longer working time than water; compatibility and re-wetting vary by product.

Book: pp. 148–149 · PDF pp. 157–158.

First body bake: what to assess

Naylor discusses low, medium and high bisque as stages of body-porcelain maturation. In the example, high bisque is the desired body stage; underfiring may leave porous, weak ceramic, while overfiring may round anatomy or produce excessive gloss.

Visual indicators vary by product and firing stage. Use them alongside the correct schedule and furnace calibration—not as an independent diagnosis.

Book: pp. 155–156 · PDF pp. 164–165.

07 / Alternative techniques · pp. 156–163

From one crown to multiple units

Chapter 8 builds on the same fundamentals for anterior alternatives, posterior crowns and an anterior fixed partial denture.

ANTERIOR

Lateral segmental

The book illustrates an alternative anterior buildup using adjacent segments of enamel and translucent porcelain to develop internal character, such as the appearance of mamelons. The design and effect are case-dependent.

POSTERIOR

Metal or porcelain occlusal

The basic porcelain steps remain similar, but posterior form and occlusal anatomy require attention. Opacious dentin or opaque modifiers may be used for internal character in the examples.

Book: pp. 156–159 · PDF pp. 165–168.

MULTIPLE UNITS

Anterior bridge

Longer working time, pontic contour and individual tooth form add complexity. The example requires careful interproximal separation before the first body bake.

Book: pp. 159–163 · PDF pp. 168–172.

Why separate the interproximal areas?

In the illustrated three-unit fixed partial denture, the book describes cutting the body porcelain through the interproximal regions down to the opaque layer so neighbouring buildups can act more independently during sintering. This helps manage shrinkage and reduce the risk of interproximal cracking.

Original concept map 1 · six connected ideas

Bonding in metal–ceramic restorations

01

1. Bonding mechanisms

  • Chemical bonding is generally regarded as predominant.
  • Mechanical retention, compression and weak van der Waals attraction also contribute.

2. The oxide / interface

  • A suitable, adherent interface supports bonding; excessive or poorly adherent oxide can weaken it.
  • Conditioning and any bonding agent depend on the alloy and ceramic system.

3. Surface preparation

  • Finish with clean tools; remove debris and contamination.
  • Use the specified abrasion and cleaning process; handle the surface without contamination.

4. Thermal compatibility

  • Choose compatible alloy and porcelain: the alloy CTE is generally slightly higher.
  • Geometry, mismatch and cooling affect residual stresses.

5. Success factors

  • Confirm alloy–ceramic compatibility, wetting, opaque application and firing schedule.
  • Design a rigid supporting framework and allow adequate veneer thickness.

6. Causes of failure

  • Contamination, poor wetting, inappropriate oxide, incompatible expansion or firing errors can impair attachment.
  • Investigate the specific cause before correcting the work.

Book: ch. 6, pp. 109–112 (PDF pp. 118–121).

Original concept map 2 · support, contour and fit

Essentials of metal–ceramic substructure design

02

1. Thickness and rigidity

  • Provide enough alloy thickness for rigidity while preserving space for the veneer.
  • The original 0.3–0.5 mm coping values are teaching examples, not universal specifications.

2. Veneer support

  • Support the ceramic with a tooth-shaped framework and avoid large unsupported areas.
  • Aim for appropriate, reasonably uniform porcelain thickness; follow the specific system.

3. Anatomy and rounded angles

  • Use anatomical contour and rounded transitions; sharp internal angles concentrate stress.
  • Support cusps and incisal edges without excessive metal bulk.

4. Connectors and embrasures

  • Connectors join retainers and pontics; design for rigidity, access and hygiene.
  • Dimensions depend on alloy, span, location and loading—not a universal 9 mm² minimum.

5. Marginal fit

  • Assess complete seating and adaptation on the die before porcelain application.
  • The margin and finish-line design depend on the prescription and preparation.

6. Occlusion and clearance

  • Ensure sufficient space for the prescribed metal and ceramic.
  • Preserve intended occlusal guidance while identifying and correcting harmful interferences.

Book: ch. 4, pp. 65–83 and ch. 1, p. 13 (PDF pp. 74–92, 22).

Original concept map 3 · interpreted as an IPS Style Ceram metal–ceramic example

Anterior ceramic layering: Ivoclar example

03

1. Framework preparation

  • Check seating and fit; clean and condition the metal as required by the alloy and ceramic system.
  • Use the prescribed shade and photographs where provided.

2. Opaque ceramic

  • Apply controlled opaque layers to support bonding, mask metal and begin the shade.
  • Fire according to the selected system and avoid pooling.

3. Dentin / body buildup

  • Build the principal body and colour, considering contour and expected shrinkage.
  • Cut back selectively to make space for the enamel and effect materials.

4. Enamel and effects

  • Add enamel or translucent materials where the intended appearance requires them.
  • Use effects such as mamelons or halo judiciously to match the case.

5. Firing and correction

  • In IPS Style Ceram, dentin and incisal can be layered before a combined firing.
  • Check shrinkage and contour; correct and refire when indicated by the system.

6. Finishing and verification

  • Finish and polish or glaze as specified.
  • Check fit, surface, contacts, occlusion and shade against the prescription.

Book: ch. 8, pp. 143–155 (PDF pp. 152–164). IPS Style Ceram is a system-specific example; verify its current IFU.

37 questions
Materials and shade · 01What are the three main functions of opaque porcelain?

It helps establish porcelain–metal attachment, masks the metal colour and begins development of the selected shade. Apply and fire it as directed by the chosen system.

Book: Ch. 8, p. 143 (PDF p. 152).

Materials and shade · 02What is the difference between dentin and enamel porcelain?

Dentin or body porcelain provides much of the body colour and anatomical volume. Enamel porcelain adds translucency and appearance; it can be used wherever required, not only at the incisal edge.

Book: pp. 143–148 · PDF pp. 152–157.

Materials and shade · 03What is a dentin cutback and why is it done?

A dentin cutback selectively removes part of the unfired dentin buildup so enamel or effect porcelain can be added without excessive contour. Its extent depends on the desired appearance and prescription.

Book: Ch. 8, pp. 150–151 (PDF pp. 159–160).

Materials and shade · 04Why should opaque porcelain not pool?

A pooled opaque layer may be excessively thick and irregular, reducing space for the body porcelain and increasing the likelihood of problems such as cracks during firing. Follow the specific system’s application guidance.

Book: Ch. 8, pp. 143–144 (PDF pp. 152–153).

Equipment and furnace · 05Name three furnace-loading designs and describe them.

Front-loading introduces work into a horizontal chamber; vertical-loading raises a platform into the chamber; a clamshell chamber closes over work that remains on its platform.

Book: Ch. 8, pp. 140–141 (PDF pp. 149–150).

Equipment and furnace · 06What is the role of a thermocouple and controller?

The thermocouple senses furnace temperature; the controller runs the selected temperature and timing programme. Correct calibration and the manufacturer’s operating instructions are important.

Book: Ch. 8, pp. 140–141 (PDF pp. 149–150).

Equipment and furnace · 07List the main elements of a porcelain firing schedule.

Identify the entry temperature and drying period, heating rate, final temperature, hold time, vacuum settings and cooling instructions. Actual values depend on the material, alloy and furnace.

Book: Ch. 8, pp. 140–141 (PDF pp. 149–150).

Equipment and furnace · 08Why is porcelain commonly fired under vacuum?

The prescribed reduced-pressure stage helps control porosity and improves optical and physical outcomes. It cannot compensate reliably for poor handling and does not guarantee a pore-free restoration.

Book: pp. 140–141, 154–155 · PDF pp. 149–150, 163–164.

Equipment and furnace · 09What may happen if a wet buildup is exposed to sudden high heat?

Rapid heating may turn liquid into steam and disturb or dislodge parts of the buildup. Use the manufacturer’s pre-drying and entry sequence.

Book: pp. 143–148 · PDF pp. 152–157.

Condensation · 10Define porcelain condensation and give its objectives.

Condensation means producing a more closely packed unfired porcelain buildup. It helps remove excess free liquid and reduce trapped air, increasing initial density and helping limit porosity and firing shrinkage.

Book: Ch. 8, pp. 141–142 (PDF pp. 150–151).

Condensation · 11List Naylor’s five condensation methods.

Capillary action, vibration, spatulation, whipping and dry powder addition. The textbook describes ultrasonic condensers as devices used for vibration, rather than as a sixth main method.

Book: Ch. 8, pp. 142–143 (PDF pp. 151–152).

Condensation · 12How do vibration and capillary action work together?

Gentle vibration helps bring liquid from the buildup toward the surface. Absorbent tissue or gauze then draws surface liquid away by capillary action; the actions may be repeated carefully as appropriate.

Book: Ch. 8, p. 142 (PDF p. 151).

Condensation · 13What is whipping?

Whipping is a brush-based variation of vibration. The brush motion encourages liquid to move to the surface where it can be blotted; excessive movement may disturb or mix layers.

Book: Ch. 8, p. 143 (PDF p. 152).

Condensation · 14What is dry powder addition?

Dry porcelain powder is applied to areas of wet porcelain and becomes wetted by liquid already present in the buildup. The book notes that accurate placement and amount can be challenging for beginners.

Book: Ch. 8, p. 143 (PDF p. 152).

Condensation · 15Why does the textbook caution beginners about spatulation?

Patting or rubbing with a spatula can move liquid to the surface, but excessive pressure can dislodge the fragile buildup or separate layers. The book favours mastering gentle vibration and blotting first.

Book: Ch. 8, p. 143 (PDF p. 152).

Voids and firing · 16How do voids affect a porcelain restoration?

Voids are defects that may concentrate stress and contribute to cracks or reduced strength. They also scatter light, which can make the porcelain appear cloudier and less translucent.

Book: Ch. 8, p. 142 (PDF p. 151).

Voids and firing · 17What are possible causes of porosity?

Trapped air, unsuitable powder–liquid handling, inadequate condensation, contamination and inappropriate drying or firing may contribute. A particular defect needs investigation rather than a single assumed cause.

Book: Ch. 8, pp. 141–143 (PDF pp. 150–152).

Voids and firing · 18What is porcelain firing shrinkage?

As particles bond and the porcelain densifies during firing, the buildup reduces in size; the book says shrinkage tends toward bulk. Better condensation can reduce the amount of shrinkage.

Book: Ch. 8, p. 142 (PDF p. 151).

Voids and firing · 19What is the difference between drying and sintering?

Drying removes free liquid before the main firing. During sintering the particles bond and the ceramic densifies; porcelain systems may involve a softened or liquid glass phase.

Book: pp. 143–148 · PDF pp. 152–157.

Voids and firing · 20What does the book mean by low, medium and high bisque?

These are stages of body-porcelain maturation. Low and medium bisque are less mature; Naylor describes high bisque as the desired body maturation stage in the context discussed. Appearance and intended endpoint still depend on the system and firing phase.

Book: Ch. 8, p. 155 (PDF p. 164).

Voids and firing · 21What features can suggest overfiring?

In the book’s first body-bake discussion, excessive gloss, loss of surface texture, slumping or rounded contours can suggest overfiring. Confirm against the particular material’s visual and processing guidance.

Book: Ch. 8, p. 155 (PDF p. 164).

Instruments · 22Which brush is often called the whipping brush?

The book describes a large, soft no. 10 brush as a whipping brush, useful for smoothing and assisting condensation. Other brushes are chosen by task and size.

Book: pp. 137–138 · PDF pp. 146–147.

Instruments · 23Why can a glass rod be useful for mixing porcelain?

Naylor notes that porcelain particles can abrade some metal mixing tools and introduce metallic contamination. A glass rod is one way to avoid this; use the instrument and procedure specified for the material.

Book: pp. 138–139 · PDF pp. 147–148.

Instruments · 24What are the functions of a hemostat and a condensing mallet?

A hemostat or suitable holder secures the framework during application and condensation. A small mallet can be used to tap a working cast gently and produce vibration.

Book: pp. 139–140 · PDF pp. 148–149.

Opaque · 25What checks come before applying opaque porcelain?

Confirm that the framework is properly finished, clean and conditioned or oxidised as prescribed; check any post-oxidation requirements, protect the prepared surface from contamination and verify fit.

Book: pp. 143–146 · PDF pp. 152–155.

Opaque · 26How can opaque porcelain be applied?

The chapter describes applying opaque with a brush or glass rod and also discusses spray and premixed opaques. The chosen application and firing approach must match the specific product.

Book: pp. 143–147 · PDF pp. 152–156.

Opaque · 27Why is excess opaque liquid handled differently from body porcelain?

Opaque is a comparatively thin layer and indiscriminate surface blotting may lift or disturb it. The book illustrates gently contacting an edge with absorbent material when liquid removal is needed.

Book: p. 145 · PDF p. 154.

Opaque · 28Why inspect the intaglio before returning the opaque coping to the die?

Residual porcelain or debris inside the coping can prevent seating. Inspect and clean as appropriate rather than forcing the restoration and damaging the die.

Book: p. 146 · PDF p. 155.

Mix and buildup · 29How does a porcelain mix behave if it is too wet or too dry?

A very wet mix is difficult to shape and may slump; a dry mix can be difficult to blend and can trap air between additions. The correct consistency depends on the material and technique.

Book: pp. 148–149 · PDF pp. 157–158.

Mix and buildup · 30Why must opaque and body mixes be kept separate?

Opaque residue can contaminate body porcelain and change the appearance or handling of the mix. Clean dispensing tools and use fresh cleaning water as appropriate.

Book: pp. 147–148 · PDF pp. 156–157.

Mix and buildup · 31What sequence is used for the illustrated anterior crown body buildup?

Prepare separate mixes, build dentin, selectively cut back for enamel, add and blend enamel/effects, condense, clean unwanted material, support on the tray and fire with the specified cycle.

Book: pp. 147–155 · PDF pp. 156–164.

Mix and buildup · 32What is the lateral segmental buildup technique?

It is an illustrated anterior alternative in which adjacent segments of enamel and translucent porcelain are used to develop internal character and incisal appearance. Details depend on the intended case.

Book: pp. 156–157 · PDF pp. 165–166.

Mix and buildup · 33Why shape posterior occlusal anatomy before the first body bake?

Developing the desired anatomy in the condensed, unfired porcelain can reduce the amount of gross grinding and correction required after firing.

Book: p. 158 · PDF p. 167.

Bridges · 34Why are multi-unit buildups more challenging than single crowns?

Several units must be kept workable and correctly contoured over a longer working period; pontic surfaces, interproximal anatomy and support during firing also need attention.

Book: pp. 159–163 · PDF pp. 168–172.

Bridges · 35Why cut the interproximal body porcelain to the opaque layer in the illustrated bridge?

The cuts individualise the adjacent buildups so each can sinter more independently, helping manage shrinkage and reducing the risk of interproximal cracks.

Book: pp. 159–163 · PDF pp. 168–172.

Bridges · 36What should be checked before firing the three-unit example?

Inspect the buildup from multiple views, clean unwanted porcelain from intaglio and exposed metal, check the interproximal separations and contacts, and balance the framework securely on the firing tray.

Book: pp. 159–162 · PDF pp. 168–171.

The chapter presents opaque application and initial body buildup and firing. Evaluation, further correction bakes and finishing are developed in Chapter 9.
