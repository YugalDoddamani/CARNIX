02 / pp. 164–169

Begin with fit, not grinding

The first body bake approximates the final form; review the restoration systematically before making changes.

The first inspection

  1. Inspect the intaglio: use magnification to look for porcelain particles or debris that could prevent seating.
  2. Confirm seating on the die: do not force the restoration; protect the master die.
  3. Check proximal contacts: use a suitable thin disclosing medium and assess the cast and adjacent surfaces.
  4. Review form: compare dimensions, contour and anatomy with the prescription, casts and available photographs.
  5. Identify deficiencies: distinguish areas needing addition from those requiring controlled reduction.

Book: pp. 167–169 · PDF pp. 176–178.

The finishing armamentarium

  • Handpiece and abrasives: used for controlled contouring and finishing.
  • Gauges: compare dimensions and help check contour.
  • Thin articulating film / disclosing media: help locate contacts or seating interferences.
  • Magnification: helps inspect the intaglio, margins and porcelain–metal junction.
  • Polishing wheels and agents: chosen for the ceramic, metal and desired surface finish.
Protect the material: use suitable extraction or wet methods, eye protection and task-specific PPE. Avoid prolonged local pressure and heat; follow the product IFU and lab SOP.

Book: pp. 164–168 · PDF pp. 173–177.

03 / pp. 170–173

Correct, refine, recheck

Work in stages, and reassess after every addition or firing.

Clinical-laboratory sequence

1 · IdentifyAssess the first bake for missing or excess contour, contacts and fit.
2 · CorrectAdd appropriate body porcelain where required; condense and fire per the system.
3 · RecheckAfter cooling, reassess seating and proximal contacts; additions can change them.
4 · ShapeReduce only where needed, moving towards finer finishing materials.
5 · OcclusionCheck contacts and interferences against the prescribed occlusal scheme.
6 · TextureUse photographs and adjacent anatomy to guide surface features.
7 · JunctionInspect the porcelain–metal transition and margins under magnification.
8 · PrepareFinish necessary adjustments before characterisation and final surface finishing.

Book: pp. 168–173 · PDF pp. 177–182. Exact steps and firing schedules depend on the selected system.

Coarse → fine, not force → damage

  • Use the least aggressive tool capable of the task; gross reduction may require a coarser instrument.
  • Progress to finer abrasives to remove scratches from earlier steps.
  • Use light, intermittent contact; do not concentrate heat in one area.
  • Keep ceramic abrasives separate from metal abrasives to minimise cross-contamination.
  • Guard thin porcelain and delicate margins.

Book: pp. 165–166, 171–173 · PDF pp. 174–175, 180–182.

Common mistakes to catch

  • Fitting on a damaged master die: may mask a real marginal problem.
  • Assuming a contact is unchanged: an additional bake can alter proximal contact.
  • Overadjusting ceramic: may expose opaque or compromise the veneer.
  • Ignoring the prescription: contact and occlusal scheme are case-specific.
  • Grinding after glaze: can damage a previously finished surface and require re-finishing.

Book: pp. 168–173 · PDF pp. 177–182.

04 / pp. 173–176

Colour is three questions

Describe what is wrong before attempting a correction.

Hue · value · chroma

Schematic colour swatches—not an actual dental shade guide. Display and lighting alter appearance.

Book: pp. 173–174 · PDF pp. 182–183.

Communicate, don’t guess

  • Hue: colour family.
  • Value: relative lightness or brightness.
  • Chroma: strength or saturation of the hue.
  • Lighting and information: photographs, shade tabs, case notes and clinician communication help interpretation; screens may differ.
  • Limits: minor surface colour changes may be possible; excessive stain can reduce translucency and major discrepancies may require a different correction.
The book distinguishes additive mixing of light from subtractive mixing of pigments. They are not interchangeable.

Book: pp. 173–176 · PDF pp. 182–185.

05 / pp. 176–182

Characterise, glaze & polish

Surface staining and glazing are related but distinct processes.

Stain versus glaze

  • Stain: adds controlled surface character or makes minor colour modifications.
  • Natural glaze: heat treatment develops a glassy finish on the porcelain surface without adding a separate glaze layer.
  • Overglaze: a separate glaze material is applied and fired using that product’s schedule.
  • Polishing: mechanical finishing can produce a smooth surface; the chosen method must suit the ceramic and instructions.

Natural glaze is also called self-glaze; overglaze is also called applied glaze in older terminology.

Book: pp. 176–180 · PDF pp. 185–189.

Make a deliberate choice

Confirm form→Clean→Stain if needed→Glaze / polish→Inspect

Assess the desired texture and gloss against the case. Avoid covering faults with stain or glaze. Use the current ceramic and stain manufacturer’s drying, firing and cooling instructions; the book’s illustrations are not a universal furnace schedule.

Book: pp. 176–182 · PDF pp. 185–191.

Protect the porcelain–metal junction

Finish the transition carefully and avoid transferring metal contamination to ceramic. After glaze, metal polishing must not mar the ceramic finish. Remove residual polishing materials using the approved cleaning procedure, then re-inspect.

Book: pp. 172–173, 181–182 · PDF pp. 181–182, 190–191.

07 / pp. 187–190

Natural variation & delivery

The final goal is a functional restoration that belongs with the patient’s natural dentition.

Observe the individual tooth

Compare outline, line angles, translucency, surface texture and gloss. Multiple photographs from different angles can communicate features that a cast or single photograph may not capture.

Book: pp. 187–189 · PDF pp. 196–198.

Corrections and hand-off

The book discusses low-fusing add-on porcelain for limited corrections; significant defects may need a more substantial body-porcelain correction. Follow the system IFU, inspect the result, remove residual polishing material, follow infection-control procedures and package the restoration and dies securely.

Book: pp. 189–190 · PDF pp. 198–199.

Assessment · 01Why is further work needed after the first body bake?

The initial bake approximates the final contours and undergoes shrinkage. Fit, contacts, occlusion, anatomy and surface appearance commonly need evaluation and sometimes correction.

Book: p. 164 · PDF 173.

Assessment · 02Why inspect the intaglio before seating?

Fired particles or debris inside the restoration can interfere with full seating. Magnification can help identify small or translucent particles.

Book: p. 168 · PDF 177.

Assessment · 03Why should a restoration not be forced onto the master die?

Forcing it can damage the die or mask the actual cause of interference. Inspect the intaglio and contacts, and correct the cause using the approved procedure.

Book: p. 168 · PDF 177.

Assessment · 04What is the function of disclosing media?

A suitable thin disclosing medium helps identify seating or contact interferences. Thickness and correct interpretation matter.

Book: p. 167–168 · PDF 176–177.

Equipment · 05Why use gauges when finishing?

Gauges help compare length, width and other relevant dimensions against the cast or reference, helping assess form and symmetry.

Book: p. 165–166 · PDF 174–175.

Equipment · 06Why avoid extended pressure with a rotary instrument?

Prolonged contact and pressure can generate damaging heat and stresses in the ceramic. Use controlled, light intermittent contact and follow the material instructions.

Book: p. 165 · PDF 174.

Equipment · 07Why dedicate abrasives to ceramic or metal?

Using an abrasive interchangeably can transfer metal particles to the ceramic and contribute to contamination or discoloration.

Book: p. 166 · PDF 175.

Correction · 08What is a second body bake used for?

Additional dentin, enamel or other compatible body porcelain can be used to correct deficiencies identified after the first bake. The second firing follows the material system’s schedule.

Book: p. 170 · PDF 179.

Correction · 09Why should contacts be checked again after a correction bake?

New porcelain and shrinkage can change proximal form and contact, so seating, contour and contacts should be reassessed after firing.

Book: p. 170–171 · PDF 179–180.

Correction · 10Why delay final glaze until required corrections are identified?

Identifying contour or contact deficiencies first allows corrections before the final surface finish and avoids unnecessarily damaging or repeating a glaze.

Book: p. 189–190 · PDF 198–199.

Correction · 11What is an add-on porcelain used for?

A compatible low-fusing add-on may be used for limited corrections such as a light contact. Its composition and firing must follow the specific system instructions.

Book: p. 189–190 · PDF 198–199.

Correction · 12Are add-on porcelains a universal solution for a major defect?

No. The book distinguishes minor add-on corrections from substantial deficiencies that may require an appropriate body-porcelain correction and additional processing.

Book: p. 190 · PDF 199.

Contouring · 13Why progress from coarse to fine during finishing?

Coarser instruments can remove material rapidly but leave scratches. Subsequent finer steps smooth the surface and remove scratches from the earlier stage.

Book: p. 171–172 · PDF 180–181.

Contouring · 14What should guide adjustment of occlusion?

The clinician’s prescription and selected occlusal scheme guide evaluation of functional contacts and harmful interferences. Do not assume every lateral contact is unwanted.

Book: p. 171 · PDF 180.

Contouring · 15Why is porcelain–metal junction finishing important?

Overextended porcelain, roughness or an irregular transition can compromise the final surface; aggressive grinding after glazing may damage the finish.

Book: p. 172–173 · PDF 181–182.

Contouring · 16What is the risk of overadjusting body porcelain?

Excessive reduction may expose opaque, compromise anatomy and surface quality, and require further correction. Opaque is not a substitute for a suitable final body porcelain surface.

Book: p. 172 · PDF 181.

Contouring · 17Why can marginal finishing on the master die be risky?

Abrasive contact may alter the gypsum die, giving a misleading impression of marginal fit. Protect the master die and use the laboratory’s approved procedure.

Book: p. 173 · PDF 182.

Colour · 18Define hue.

Hue is the colour family: it helps distinguish one colour from another.

Book: p. 173–174 · PDF 182–183.

Colour · 19Define value.

Value describes relative lightness or brightness: high value is lighter or brighter than low value.

Book: p. 173–174 · PDF 182–183.

Colour · 20Define chroma.

Chroma is the strength, intensity or saturation of a hue.

Book: p. 174 · PDF 183.

Colour · 21How do additive and subtractive colour systems differ?

Additive mixing concerns light or illuminants; subtractive mixing concerns pigments or stains. Their primaries and mixing behaviour should not be treated as interchangeable.

Book: p. 175–176 · PDF 184–185.

Colour · 22What is the role of a porcelain surface stain?

Surface stains can add character or make minor shade modifications, depending on the system and clinical instructions. They cannot reliably solve every major colour discrepancy.

Book: p. 173–176 · PDF 182–185.

Colour · 23Why can excessive surface staining be undesirable?

Too much stain can impede light transmission and reduce the natural translucency of the porcelain.

Book: p. 173 · PDF 182.

Glazing · 24What is natural or self-glaze?

Natural glaze is a heat treatment that develops a glassy surface on the porcelain without applying a separate glaze layer, using the relevant material’s instructions.

Book: p. 177–179 · PDF 186–188.

Glazing · 25What is an overglaze?

An overglaze uses a separate glaze material applied to the restoration and fired according to the product instructions. It is also called an applied glaze in older terminology.

Book: p. 177–180 · PDF 186–189.

Glazing · 26Are staining and glazing the same procedure?

No. Stains modify surface colour or character, while glazing develops a surface finish; they can be performed together in a system-specific final firing.

Book: p. 176–180 · PDF 185–189.

Glazing · 27What is the purpose of ceramic polishing?

Polishing is a mechanical finishing process that produces an appropriate smooth surface using compatible instruments and a progressive sequence suitable for the ceramic.

Book: p. 180–181 · PDF 189–190.

Cases · 28What should be checked on a posterior porcelain occlusal surface?

Evaluate occlusal anatomy and the prescribed functional contacts, as well as proximal contacts, contour and areas requiring porcelain addition.

Book: p. 183–184 · PDF 192–193.

Cases · 29What additional areas require attention on a bridge?

Assess the pontic tissue surface, proximal contacts, individual contours, gingival embrasures and hygiene access, following the prescribed design.

Book: p. 184–186 · PDF 193–195.

Cases · 30Why request more than one clinical photograph?

Different views can show outline, texture, translucency and gloss more clearly; a single image or gypsum cast may not convey all the patient-specific features.

Book: p. 187–189 · PDF 196–198.

Delivery · 31What should be done before the completed case is returned?

Inspect fit and finish, remove residues using approved methods, follow applicable infection-control procedures and protect the restoration and dies during packaging.

Book: p. 190 · PDF 199.
