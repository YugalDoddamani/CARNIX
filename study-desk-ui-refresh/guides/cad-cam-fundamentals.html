1.1

History of dental CAD/CAM

PDF pp. 14–34 · From industrial automation to patient-specific dentistry.

CAD/CAM combines three broad operations: acquiring data, using software to analyse and design, and manufacturing the proposed object. Industrial automation inspired dental applications, but a dental restoration differs from a mass-produced part: each must fit a particular patient and prescription.

Industrial CAD/CAM→Duret's early dental concept→Mörmann's chairside CEREC work→Andersson / Procera approaches→Connected clinical, lab & milling workflows

The book discusses early single-unit chairside work and centralized production of copings, then expansion into broader restorative and prosthetic applications. Early hardware and software limitations contrast with the later move toward 3D design and distributed collaboration.

RememberTechnology can reduce some manual steps, but it does not remove the need for diagnosis, clear records, material selection or verification.
1.2

Digital scanning & applications

PDF pp. 35–63 · Capture surfaces and their relationships.

A scanner acquires surface data; software processes and combines it into a digital model. Intraoral scanning captures accessible surfaces in the mouth. A laboratory scanner digitizes a compatible cast, die or impression outside the mouth.

Capture principles and scanner choices

Optical systems project or use light and process images from different viewpoints to create a surface representation. The book describes triangulation, wavefront sampling, optical sectioning and confocal approaches. Systems differ in capture method, tip size, software, colour capability and powder requirements; these features should be assessed for the device, not assumed from its category. Benchtop systems can digitize impressions and casts; intraoral systems avoid the impression-to-cast transfer for suitable cases.

Trueness is closeness to the actual geometry; precision is repeatability of measurements. A repeatable scan can still be systematically wrong. Longer scan spans, difficult access and stitching may affect performance; compare evidence for the intended indication.

What makes a scan useful?

  • Clear preparation boundaries, adjacent anatomy and relevant soft tissues.
  • Opposing arch and a usable bite registration when required for design.
  • Complete coverage without obvious holes, misalignment or distortion.
  • A file that the receiving software can actually interpret.

The book discusses digital impressions for indirect restorations, treatment planning, records and occlusal analysis. They can be reviewed and transferred electronically, but accuracy still depends on visibility, capture technique, equipment, anatomy and the specific application. Digital occlusal information supplements clinical evaluation; it does not replace it.

Capture prepared arch→Capture opposing arch & bite→Inspect and rescan gaps→Transfer securely→Design / analyse
1.3

Preparation & tissue visibility

PDF pp. 64–86 · The scanner and the milling tool both have limits.

If the preparation margin is hidden by tissue, blood, saliva or other material, the scanner may not capture it reliably. The book links visible margins and tissue management to the quality of digital impressions. Preparation design also needs to respect the restorative material and the geometry of the intended milling tools.

  • Ensure appropriate material clearance and preparation form according to the restorative system.
  • Very sharp internal features may be difficult to reproduce with a rounded milling tool.
  • Inspect the scan at magnification and recapture unclear regions when feasible.
  • Gingival retraction, haemostasis and tooth preparation are clinical responsibilities; methods depend on patient factors and professional judgement.
Do not infer a universal specificationPreparation, reduction and tissue-management choices are case- and material-specific; the book is not a substitute for clinical training or current instructions.
1.4

Designing restorations

PDF pp. 87–102 · Turn records into a controlled restorative proposal.

The book compares conventional diagnostic wax-ups with digital proposals. CAD software may use tooth libraries, a contralateral tooth, an existing restoration or a scanned approved provisional as an anatomical reference. The operator must still judge form and adapt the proposal to the case.

Critical design checks

  • Identify the margin accurately and specify the intended insertion path and internal space as required by the system.
  • Assess contour, emergence, proximal contacts, embrasures and surface characteristics.
  • Use opposing-arch and bite data to review occlusal relationships; virtual articulation is a model, not a substitute for clinical evaluation.
  • Check minimum material thickness and connector dimensions according to the selected material and indication.
Verify scans & prescription→Mark margins→Choose anatomical proposal→Adjust form, contacts & occlusion→Check material / machine constraints→Approve design

1.6

Manufacturing and milling

PDF pp. 134–153 · Match production to design and material.

Subtractive

Milling cuts a design from a compatible block or disc. Tool geometry, machine axes, tool wear, material properties and nesting affect what can be produced.

Additive

Printing builds a part in successive layers. The book discusses approaches including photopolymer processes and powder-based techniques; post-processing depends on the process.

How printing processes differ

  • SLA: light selectively cures liquid photopolymer; compatible washing and curing are part of the validated process.
  • Powder-bed laser processes: energy selectively bonds or melts powder layers. Metal and polymer systems differ in materials, processing and safety controls.
  • Fused deposition modelling: heated thermoplastic is extruded as layers.
  • Robocasting: printable paste or slurry is deposited in a programmed shape; ceramic parts may require further processing.

Print orientation, supports, layer thickness, machine calibration and post-processing can influence the finished part. Choose a validated material–machine–process combination for the intended use.

When comparing machines, consider supported materials, restoration size, tool access, precision for the intended task, wet/dry capability, throughput, maintenance, compatibility and total cost—not just the number of axes.

Validated design→Choose material & route→Generate toolpath / slice→Manufacture→Material-specific post-processing→Fit, surface & quality checks
2.1

Workflow variations & interoperability

PDF pp. 155–184 · Decide where each part of the work happens.

The book compares chairside, laboratory and collaborative workflows. Some systems use proprietary formats or restricted connections; more open systems can exchange data between compatible products. Open format does not guarantee interoperability: supported data types, versions, licensing and transfer requirements must still be checked.

Compare practical routes

  • Chairside: capture, design and manufacture within the practice when equipment and case selection allow. Efficient for suitable work, but requires training and investment.
  • Laboratory-based: a practice sends digital files or conventional records to a laboratory; the laboratory designs and/or arranges production.
  • Distributed / collaborative: clinicians, laboratory and manufacturing centres share compatible records and assigned tasks. Version control, prescription clarity and acceptance checks matter.

File splitting

In the book, file splitting means separating a coordinated digital design into component files—for example, a framework and an outer restoration—so they can be fabricated by different processes or locations and then assembled. The benefit is coordinated planning; fit and material compatibility still require verification.

Shared case data→Coordinated multi-part design→Separate component files→Manufacture by suitable routes→Verify assembly

STL commonly represents surface geometry, not a complete patient record; other formats and metadata may be needed for colour or case information.

2.2

CBCT and integration

PDF pp. 185–229 · Understand volume data, image quality and registration.

Cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) uses X-ray projections and reconstruction software to create a three-dimensional image volume. A voxel is a volume element in that dataset; a surface mesh is a different representation of geometry.

Factors affecting image quality

  • Field of view, acquisition settings and reconstruction choices.
  • Noise, patient movement and artifacts, including metal streaking and beam hardening.
  • Voxel size: smaller does not automatically mean the best diagnostic choice; image quality, purpose and exposure must be considered.

Segmentation separates structures from a volume, and surface models can be aligned with intraoral or facial scans for planning. Registration and segmentation errors can propagate into a guide or design. CBCT imaging requires clinical justification, appropriate exposure and professional interpretation; it is not routinely needed for every CAD/CAM restoration.

Clinically justified acquisition→Reconstruct volume→Inspect image quality & artifacts→Segment / generate surface when appropriate→Register optical data→Verify alignment before planning
2.3

Same-day dentistry

PDF pp. 230–248 · A chairside pathway when appropriate.

Clinical assessment & preparation→Scan and review→Chairside design→Mill / process material→Finish, fit & deliver

Potential benefits include fewer appointments and direct control over the workflow. Challenges include case selection, scan visibility, equipment cost, operator training, material-specific processing time and fit or aesthetic corrections. Not all cases are suited to one appointment; lab collaboration remains valuable.

2.4

Anterior full-coverage restorations

PDF pp. 249–278 · Integrate aesthetics, communication and function.

The book contrasts analog wax-ups combined with digital manufacture against fully digital planning. Approved provisional or reference contours can be scanned and used to guide final design where clinically suitable.

  • Record facial and dental context, shade and desired tooth position.
  • Develop and review a diagnostic design with the clinician and patient.
  • Evaluate proportions, midline, incisal edge, surface texture, contacts and occlusion.
  • Preserve and communicate the approved design; verify final appearance and fit.
Exam distinctionCopying an existing shape is useful only when that shape has been assessed as an appropriate reference. Digital duplication does not make an unsuitable design correct.
2.5

Implant treatment

PDF pp. 279–322 · Plan the restoration and implant position together.

Digital records may include oral scans, photographs and clinically justified radiographic imaging. The book discusses collaborative planning, virtual tooth arrangement, implant placement planning, abutment and prosthetic design, and guided workflows.

Clinical diagnosis & records→Restorative goal / virtual wax-up→Combine and verify relevant image data→Plan implant and prosthesis→Guide or provisional if indicated→Definitive components & verification

Prosthetic workflow and verification

Following implant placement and appropriate clinical healing, a correctly seated compatible scan body may communicate implant position to the design software through its matching digital library. The technician checks implant system, component selection, seating information, access, restorative space and prescription before designing the abutment and restoration. A guide or digital plan does not ensure the final implant position exactly matches the plan; subsequent clinical verification is essential.

Accuracy depends on each link: image quality, registration, guide fit, system-compatible scan bodies and libraries, manufacturing and clinical execution. The design must allow appropriate support, occlusion, screw access where applicable and patient hygiene.

2.6

Digital denture fabrication

PDF pp. 323–369 · Digital manufacturing still needs clinical records.

The book emphasizes that software cannot replace the essential clinical information used to design a complete denture: functional ridge form, jaw relationship, vertical dimension, occlusal plane orientation and midline. A plan also considers tooth position, aesthetics, phonetics and patient review.

Obtain clinical records→Digitize / integrate records→Design base & tooth arrangement→Manufacture prototype if indicated→Clinical try-in & modifications→Produce definitive denture→Clinical delivery & review

Common sources of difficulty

  • Inadequate anatomy: a scan or impression must capture the relevant supporting tissues; movable tissues can be challenging.
  • Incorrect records: a polished digital setup cannot correct an incorrect jaw relation or vertical dimension by itself.
  • Communication: review proposed teeth, contours and patient feedback before final manufacture.
  • Assembly: check bonding of denture teeth or system-specific integration and inspect the final prosthesis.

Printed or milled devices can support selected stages, but suitability depends on the system and clinical requirements. Preserved digital designs may support later replication if records remain accurate and the prosthesis remains appropriate.

2.7

Digital orthodontics

PDF pp. 370–407 · Digital setup supports, but does not replace, orthodontic diagnosis.

Clinical diagnosis & treatment objectives→Digital models→Virtual staging & clinical review→Manufacture models / appliances→Clinical delivery, monitoring & revisions

The book discusses aligner planning, limitations and patient cooperation. It presents a 2018 view of aligner biomechanics; do not treat historical generalizations as universal rules about what current systems can or cannot accomplish. Movement predictability, attachments, refinements, patient adherence and suitability depend on the case and current evidence.

2.8

CAD/CAM in dental education

PDF pp. 408–441 · Digital feedback can make practice more targeted.

The book describes scanning student preparations and comparing them with a defined reference, including colour-coded indications of over- or under-reduction. It also discusses preclinical and clinical exposure to digital dentistry and student responses.

Use feedback to identify where to practise, but recognize that a comparison is only as useful as its reference, scan quality and assessment criteria. Faculty supervision and clinical judgement remain important.

Prepare typodont tooth→Scan work→Compare with reference→Review feedback with instructor→Practise and reassess
2.9

Additional considerations

PDF pp. 442–461 · One clinical goal can have several manufacturing paths.

The book illustrates different ways to produce a fixed three-unit zirconia bridge: a zirconia substructure with a pressed veneer, a full-contour zirconia design, and a zirconia framework with a separately manufactured lithium-disilicate overlay. These are examples in the text, not interchangeable universal recipes.

Veneered approach

Plan the framework and space for the veneer; ensure compatible components, support and an appropriate joining / firing protocol.

Full-contour approach

Manufacture the restoration in its full form and use the applicable finishing and characterization procedure.

File splitting, digital records and distributed manufacture can offer flexibility, but each transfer or assembly adds a verification step. The book anticipates greater interoperability; as a 2018 prediction, that is not proof that all modern systems are open or mutually compatible.

08

Dental CAD/CAM evolution: exam answer

Book §1.1, PDF pp. 14–34

Definition: Dental CAD/CAM uses digital data acquisition, computer-aided design and computer-aided manufacturing to produce a patient-specific restoration or device.

Sequence of development

  1. Industrial beginnings: Computer-controlled engineering and manufacturing provided the ideas and machinery later adapted to dentistry.
  2. Early dental concepts: In the 1970s François Duret proposed using CAD/CAM for individualized dental restorations.
  3. Chairside development: In the 1980s Werner Mörmann and colleagues developed CEREC, demonstrating the scan–design–mill concept for chairside restorations.
  4. Laboratory and centralized manufacture: Systems including Procera developed digital scanning and computer-assisted production through laboratory/production-centre workflows.
  5. Wider use: Digital impressions, 3D design, improved materials, milling and printing expanded the range of restorative, implant, denture and orthodontic applications.
  6. Connected workflows: Clinicians, laboratories and production centres may exchange digital case data, provided systems and file formats are compatible.
Industrial CAD/CAM→Dental concept→Chairside scanning and milling→Laboratory / central production→Integrated, multi-indication workflows

Advantages: repeatable prefabricated materials, digital storage, potentially faster communication and opportunities for planned manufacture. Limitations: investment, training, maintenance, file compatibility and errors at any stage. Digital production does not automatically ensure clinical success.

11–12

Materials and equipment by production method

Book §§1.5–1.6, PDF pp. 103–153 · Equipment details are supplementary

A. Materials for milled restorations

  • Glass-based ceramics: feldspathic, leucite-reinforced and lithium-disilicate glass ceramics; visual qualities and strength vary by composition. Some require a material-specific crystallization firing.
  • Zirconia: a polycrystalline ceramic, often milled in a presintered state and then sintered with machine/software compensation for shrinkage. Different zirconia formulations have different properties and indications.
  • Resin-composite / hybrid blocks: polymer-based materials with inorganic fillers or networks; applicable uses, finishing and bonding depend on the product.
  • PMMA and other polymers: used in selected provisional restorations and other approved applications.
  • Metal alloys: certain systems mill compatible cobalt–chromium or titanium for indicated frameworks and components.

B. Materials for milling dentures

Prefabricated PMMA discs can be used for denture bases and, in compatible systems, teeth or tooth-bearing portions. Teeth may be manufactured separately or integrated depending on the system. Bonding agents, luting materials and surface preparation must match the selected validated workflow. Record shade, material batch and denture design.

C. Materials for 3D-printed restorations

Photopolymer resins are used for models, guides, try-ins, provisionals and, where specifically authorized and supported by evidence, selected definitive indications. The book's comments reflect 2018; current materials have expanded. A printer, resin, exposure settings, cleaning and post-curing procedure must be an approved combination for the intended use. Do not assume every printed resin is suitable for intraoral use.

Digital workflow: crown and fixed partial denture

Book §§1.4, 2.1 and 2.9 · PDF pp. 87–102, 155–184, 442–461

FPD means fixed partial denture: a fixed restoration replacing one or more teeth, commonly with retainers, pontic(s) and connectors.

Prescription & records→Prepare and manage tissue→Scan or digitize→Check data / register bite→CAD margins and anatomy→CAM fabrication→Post-process & inspect→Clinical fit and delivery

Steps and checkpoints

  1. Case acceptance: confirm patient identifier, tooth or teeth, material, shade, design and prescription; resolve missing data.
  2. Capture: the clinician prepares and scans, or an impression / cast is digitized by the laboratory. Include relevant neighbouring and opposing surfaces and bite.
  3. Check: verify margins, complete coverage, scan-body seating if relevant and alignment of the arches. If unreliable, request new data.
  4. Design: mark margins, set system-specific parameters, select anatomy and inspect insertion, internal space, contacts, occlusion and thickness.
  5. For an FPD: additionally design retainers, pontic shape and hygienic tissue relationship, and connector size and position according to material and indication. Check available space and path of insertion.
  6. Manufacture: nest or position the design; mill or print in an indicated material and perform the required material-specific processing.
  7. Verify: inspect margins, fit on a suitable model when available, proximal and occlusal relationships, surface quality and shade. The clinician verifies intraoral fit and delivers it using the appropriate protocol.
Exam phraseA crown replaces the coronal form of one prepared tooth; a tooth-supported FPD joins retainers to one or more pontics using connectors. Clinical design differs by case.

Digital workflow: RPD, cast partial and complete dentures

Book §2.6, PDF pp. 323–369; removable framework details are supplementary

Abbreviations: RPD = removable partial denture; CPD is used by some courses for cast partial denture, while others use it for complete denture. Both workflows are included below.

A. RPD / cast partial denture framework

Prescription & examination→Impression / scan and model→Survey and plan path→Design rests, connectors, clasps and bases→Produce framework→Try-in and jaw records→Teeth, processing and delivery
  1. Obtain a diagnosis, prescription and accurate hard- and soft-tissue records; identify teeth and edentulous areas.
  2. Survey the physical or digital model for the path of insertion, guide planes, undercuts and interferences; agree design with the clinician.
  3. Design rests, major and minor connectors, clasps, denture-base retention and relief appropriate to the case.
  4. Manufacture a framework using an indicated route: printed or milled castable pattern followed by investing and casting, direct metal manufacture, or another validated method.
  5. Inspect and clinically verify framework fit; obtain records, arrange teeth, try in and process the bases. The clinician checks function and gives patient instructions.

B. Complete denture workflow

Impressions / tissue records→Jaw relation, vertical dimension & facial references→Digital base and tooth setup→Try-in when appropriate→Mill / print final components→Delivery and follow-up

Technology does not replace border and tissue assessment, centric relation, vertical dimension, occlusal plane, tooth position, aesthetics or patient review. Number of visits and try-in method vary with the system and case.

15

Colour coding: inlays and mock-up wax

Supplementary laboratory concept · related digital feedback: book §2.8, PDF pp. 408–441

Purpose: Use colour to distinguish working steps, material roles or anatomical zones clearly. It improves communication and makes additions and corrections easier to identify.

  • Inlay wax: contrasting colours can improve visibility of a wax pattern against the die and distinguish a pattern or wax-up from the surrounding model. Select the grade suitable for direct or indirect use; good adaptation and minimal distortion are more important than its colour.
  • Mock-up / diagnostic wax-up: different colours can distinguish existing surfaces from proposed additions, tooth groups or stages of a design. Tell the clinician what each colour means.
  • Digital colour maps: software may show over- and under-reduction, contact intensity or deviations in different colours. Read the software's legend and scale; red, green and blue do not have universal meanings.
ImportantThere is no universal dental-laboratory rule that a particular wax colour always means a particular inlay type, contour or procedure. Follow your institution's / product's colour key. A wax colour is not the final tooth shade.
16

Spruing: materials and types

Supplementary conventional casting topic

Definition: Spruing attaches a channel-forming pattern to a wax or castable resin pattern. After burnout the channel provides a route for molten alloy to enter the mould. Correct positioning and feeding support complete casting and help reduce shrinkage defects.

Materials

Common sprue formers include preformed wax rods, other suitable wax patterns and purpose-made castable plastic / resin sprues. Their burnout behaviour must be compatible with the investment and alloy system. A sprue base or crucible former shapes the entry to the mould.

Types and terms

  • Direct spruing: a sprue connects the pattern directly to the entry region; often used for appropriate small patterns.
  • Indirect / runner-bar spruing: a main sprue feeds a runner bar, which feeds individual patterns through subsidiary sprues; commonly considered for multi-unit castings.
  • Single and multiple sprues: describe the number of feed channels; selection depends on geometry, alloy and manufacturer's technique.
  • Reservoir: an enlarged region positioned in the feed system to help maintain a supply of molten alloy during solidification, where used.
  • Vent: a supplementary escape pathway used in some systems; follow the selected casting technique.
Inspect wax / resin pattern→Choose compatible sprue design→Attach to suitable bulky area→Position in ring with adequate investment→Invest and burn out

Avoid creating thin, constricted connections, sharp junctions or a design that prevents complete filling. Dimensions, reservoir placement and ring position depend on casting method, alloy and investment instructions.

17

Investing: materials, classification and equipment

Supplementary conventional casting topic

Definition: Investing surrounds a pattern with a refractory material that sets to form a mould. Burnout removes the pattern; mould expansion helps compensate for alloy casting shrinkage within the limits of the system.

Basic composition

  • Refractory: generally silica forms, such as quartz or cristobalite, provide heat resistance and thermal expansion.
  • Binder: holds the investment together after setting; binder chemistry determines classification and suitable temperature range.
  • Modifiers: adjust working, setting or thermal behaviour. Liquids and dilution requirements are product-specific.
Class by binderTypical use / caution
Gypsum-bondedTraditionally for lower-fusing dental alloys; not suitable for high-temperature casting outside its stated range.
Phosphate-bondedCommon for higher-temperature casting, including many base-metal and metal–ceramic alloys, when approved for the alloy.
Silica-bondedUsed in certain high-temperature framework casting systems; process depends on the selected product.

Equipment and sequence

Casting ring or ringless system, crucible former, suitable ring liner if specified, mixing bowl and spatula or vacuum mixer, balance / measuring equipment, vibrator where appropriate, burnout furnace, and protective equipment.

Mount and inspect pattern→Select compatible investment and ring→Measure and mix as directed→Invest without trapping air→Allow proper setting→Burnout according to instructions

Sources of error: incorrect liquid-to-powder ratio, trapped air, early disturbance, incompatible materials, faulty burnout or an incorrectly positioned pattern. Silica-containing dust is hazardous; use appropriate controls and avoid dry sweeping.

Casting: procedure and defects

Supplementary conventional casting topic

Definition: Casting forms a metal object by melting an alloy and forcing or drawing it into the mould created when a wax or castable pattern is removed.

Pattern & sprue→Invest and set→Burnout / preheat→Melt alloy→Cast into mould→Cool as specified→Divest, finish and inspect
  1. Inspect the pattern; sprue and invest using materials compatible with the prescribed alloy.
  2. Allow investment to set, then burn out and preheat according to the investment and alloy manufacturer's schedule.
  3. Prepare the approved casting machine and crucible; melt the alloy using the manufacturer's controlled method.
  4. Introduce the molten alloy using a suitable centrifugal or pressure / vacuum casting system.
  5. Allow cooling according to the alloy and investment instructions; divest, remove sprues and finish.
  6. Check completeness, internal fit, margins, porosity and surface defects before the next stage.
DefectPossible contributorsPrevention idea
Incomplete castingThin sections, unsuitable spruing, insufficient flow or wrong casting conditions.Review design and system-specific casting process.
PorosityShrinkage or gas-related causes; hot spots and unsuitable melting or feeding.Use correct alloy handling, sprue design and controlled process.
Surface nodules / roughnessAir bubbles against the pattern, investment or burnout issues.Use correct mixing and careful investment; inspect the mould process.
Poor fitPattern distortion, incorrect expansion or processing conditions.Protect pattern and follow the validated investment/alloy system.

Do not heat or handle casting equipment without training, ventilation, heat-resistant PPE and facility-specific safety controls.

Dental ceramics: classification and finishing

Book §1.5, PDF pp. 103–133; practical finishing overview is supplementary

Classification by composition

  • Glass-based ceramics: feldspathic ceramics and glass ceramics containing reinforcing crystals, such as leucite or lithium disilicate.
  • Polycrystalline ceramics: alumina and zirconia; no conventional glass phase.
  • Resin-matrix ceramic-like materials: a related but distinct group containing polymer and inorganic constituents; terminology and classifications vary.

Other classifications use processing method (layered, pressed or milled) or construction (monolithic or veneered). Choose classification basis before listing examples.

Sintering, staining, glazing and polishing

CrystallizationA heat treatment develops the intended crystalline structure in certain glass-ceramic systems.Not every glass ceramic or zirconia follows the same firing sequence.

Dental implants: components, abutments and hybrid bar

Book §2.5, PDF pp. 279–322; casting and parts detail is supplementary

Basic components

  • Implant fixture: component placed in bone, intended to support the prosthesis after appropriate healing.
  • Abutment: connects the implant or intermediate interface to the restoration; may be screw-retained or used with a cement-retained restoration, depending on design.
  • Abutment / prosthetic screw: secures a compatible component when the system uses one.
  • Prosthesis: crown, fixed bridge, bar-supported prosthesis or removable overdenture, according to the treatment plan.
  • Related transfer components: healing abutment, impression coping, laboratory analogue and scan body are used for specific clinical or laboratory stages; not every case uses each component.

Abutment types

TypeUse / distinguishing feature
Stock / prefabricatedManufactured in standard shapes and sizes; selection depends on implant system, position and available space.
CustomDesigned for the individual case, potentially by CAD/CAM, to manage emergence and restorative form.
Straight / angledDifferent geometries assist restoration of implants with different angulations; access and loading must be assessed.
Intermediate multi-unit abutmentUsed in compatible multi-unit prosthetic workflows, with straight or angled options as indicated.
Material / interface variantsTitanium, zirconia-based or hybrid designs may be used when supported by the manufacturer; interface and screw compatibility are critical.

Hybrid bar

A bar or framework can connect multiple implants and support an appropriately designed prosthesis. A fixed hybrid prosthesis generally combines an implant-supported framework with prosthetic teeth and a restorative base; a bar-retained overdenture is removable and uses attachments. Terminology varies, so specify whether the prosthesis is fixed or removable. Design requires fit, screw access, space, cleansability, occlusion and compatible components.

Cast abutment / cast-on workflow: principle

Identify implant system & prescribed component→Seat compatible analogue and castable component→Wax / design custom contour→Sprue and invest→Burn out and cast approved alloy→Finish without damaging interface→Inspect fit and send for clinical verification

A fully castable pattern and a manufacturer-approved cast-to / UCLA-type component with a prefabricated interface are not the same. Casting alloys and temperatures must be compatible with the specific component. Protect critical connection surfaces; avoid indiscriminate grinding or alteration of the implant interface. Verify with the manufacturer's instructions and prescribed torque/prosthetic protocol; implant systems are not automatically interchangeable.

Laboratory safety and infection control

Supplementary general guidance · apply current local rules and facility policies

Safety regulations and working controls

  • Risk assessment: identify hazards, use written procedures, staff training, incident reporting, equipment maintenance and safety data sheets.
  • Dust and aerosols: use source extraction and appropriate ventilation for grinding, silica-containing investment and ceramic dust; use appropriate respiratory protection where required.
  • Chemicals: label and store resins, solvents, acids and disinfectants correctly; use compatible gloves and eye protection. Prevent skin contact with uncured resin.
  • Heat and machinery: use guards, heat-resistant tools and PPE, machine maintenance, safe electrical connections and trained operators.
  • Fire and metal powders: follow manufacturer and site-specific fire and combustible-powder procedures; store and dispose of metal powder safely. Do not assume water is appropriate for a metal-powder fire.
  • Waste and records: segregate contaminated sharps, chemical waste and ordinary waste as required; protect patient data and document equipment checks.

Infection-control protocol

Receive case as potentially contaminated→Check clinic cleaning / disinfection status→Clean and disinfect safely as required→Work in designated clean area→Clean and disinfect before return→Communicate status & document
  1. Use standard precautions, hand hygiene and appropriate PPE; separate contaminated receiving and clean production areas.
  2. On receipt, check the accompanying disinfection record. Clean and disinfect impressions, appliances or prostheses using a product and method compatible with the material and effective for the stated contact time. Coordinate with the clinic to avoid gaps or harmful repeated treatment.
  3. Use dedicated containers, clean/disinfect reusable tools and touch surfaces, and process instruments by the facility's validated method; sterilize heat-stable items when required by their use and applicable policy.
  4. Prevent cross-contamination through handpieces, polishing equipment, pumice and work surfaces using local protocols.
  5. Clean and disinfect completed items before dispatch when appropriate; package safely and clearly communicate the treatment performed.
ScopeSpecific disinfectants, dilution, contact time, waste categories and legal duties depend on material, product instructions, local regulations and the facility's infection-control policy. This overview is not a substitute for those requirements.
REVIEW

Short answers & retrieval practice

Use these to test understanding across the whole book.

1. What are the three broad components of dental CAD/CAM?

Data acquisition, computer-aided design and computer-aided manufacturing. A complete workflow also requires appropriate planning, processing and verification. [1.1, PDF pp. 14–34]

2. Why are scan visibility and milling geometry important?

Hidden margins may not be captured accurately, while rounded milling tools may be unable to reproduce certain sharp internal features. Preparation and material requirements must be considered by the clinical team. [1.3, pp. 64–86]

3. What is file splitting?

It is separation of a coordinated digital design into component files that can be fabricated separately and then assembled, subject to compatibility and fit verification. [2.1, pp. 155–184]

4. How do a CBCT volume and an optical surface scan differ?

A CBCT scan is reconstructed as a 3D image volume from X-ray projections; an optical scan records visible surface geometry. They may be registered for planning, but artifacts and registration errors must be checked. [2.2, pp. 185–229]

5. What clinical records are important in digital denture planning?

Ridge form, jaw relationship, vertical dimension, occlusal plane orientation and midline are among the essential records. Tooth position, aesthetics, phonetics and clinical try-in also matter. [2.6, pp. 323–369]

6. Compare monolithic and veneered restorations.

A monolithic restoration is manufactured as the full restorative contour in its principal material; a veneered restoration has a supporting structure with an outer layer. Both require material-specific design, finishing and fit checks. [1.5 & 2.9, pp. 103–133, 442–461]

  1. Scan → design → manufacture → finish → verify.
  2. Records → digital denture design → prototype → try-in → definitive denture.
  3. Clinically justified CBCT → reconstruction → segmentation → registration → verified plan.
  4. Combined design → file splitting → separate manufacture → assembly → inspection.
