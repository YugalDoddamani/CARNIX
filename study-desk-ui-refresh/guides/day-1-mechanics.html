01

Tooth contours & alignment

Start with anatomy: every restoration must fit both the individual tooth and the arch.

What is a tooth contour?

A tooth contour is the shape and curvature of the crown surfaces. A restoration should reproduce appropriate form while supporting function, aesthetics, cleanability and healthy surrounding tissues. The intended contour depends on the tooth, its position and the patient's existing anatomy.

FeatureWhat to understandWhy it matters in a restoration
Axial contoursCurves of the facial, lingual, mesial and distal surfaces.Affects appearance, emergence from the gingiva and access for cleaning.
Height of contourThe most prominent part of a crown surface in a given view.Helps establish natural form and appropriate contact and embrasure relationships.
Contact areasAreas where adjacent teeth touch.Help maintain tooth position and protect the interdental space; overly tight or open contacts cause problems.
EmbrasuresSpaces around the contact area, described as gingival, occlusal/incisal, facial and lingual.Allow space for tissues and food passage; important for cleaning and natural appearance.
Occlusal anatomyCusps, ridges, grooves and fossae on posterior teeth.Guides contacts and movements; excessive or misplaced anatomy may interfere with function.
Incisal anatomyIncisal edges and surface form of anterior teeth.Influences aesthetics, speech and anterior guidance.

Alignment in the arch

Alignment describes how teeth are positioned and oriented in relation to one another and the dental arch. Assess tooth position, long-axis inclination, rotation, spacing, arch form, adjacent contacts and opposing teeth. A restoration must be checked in the context of the whole arch—not as an isolated tooth.

Exam point · under- vs over-contouringOver-contouring can create bulky areas that hinder cleaning and irritate tissues. Under-contouring can produce poor form or contacts. Both can compromise function and aesthetics. Aim for anatomy appropriate to the individual case, not a uniformly bulky or flat crown.

Practical check sequence

Identify tooth & arch position→Observe adjacent teeth→Form axial & occlusal anatomy→Check contacts & embrasures→Check opposing teeth & movements

Paper tip: underline contour, contact, embrasure and function in a short answer.

02

Occlusion & equilibration

Understand how teeth meet at rest and move against one another.

Definition and two ways to study it

Occlusion is the relationship between the maxillary and mandibular teeth as they contact. Study both the morphology of the contacting surfaces and the function of contacts during mandibular movements.

Morphological aspect

Concerns the form and arrangement of teeth and their occlusal surfaces: cusps, ridges, grooves, fossae, tooth position and contact relationships.

Functional aspect

Concerns how teeth contact during closure and movement, including protrusive and lateral movements, guidance and potential interferences.

Core terms

TermMeaning
Static occlusionTooth contact when the mandible is not moving, such as in maximum intercuspation.
Dynamic occlusionTooth contact relationships while the mandible moves.
Maximum intercuspationThe position of maximum intercuspation of the opposing teeth, independent of condylar position.
Anterior guidanceThe influence of the contacting anterior teeth on mandibular movement.
Occlusal interferenceAn undesirable contact that hinders a desired movement or closure pattern; interpretation is case-specific.
ArticulatorA mechanical instrument that holds maxillary and mandibular casts to simulate selected jaw relationships and movements.

Why does occlusion matter for the technician?

  • A high restoration may prevent comfortable or even closure.
  • Incorrect cusp or guidance form can introduce unwanted contacts during movement.
  • Proper cast mounting, records and communication with the clinician help relate the restoration to the patient.
  • Laboratory checks cannot replace the clinician's final assessment in the mouth.

Dental equilibration

Occlusal equilibration is the planned modification of selected occlusal contacts to achieve an intended occlusal relationship. It requires diagnosis and a case-specific treatment plan; it is not routine grinding of every high-looking contact. The syllabus includes equilibration techniques, so know its purpose and broad sequence.

Clinical assessment & diagnosis→Plan intended contacts→Mark & verify contacts→Selective adjustment by clinician, if indicated→Recheck closure & movements→Polish & reassess
Do not overstateArticulating marks help locate contacts but require interpretation; a mark alone does not establish that tooth structure should be removed. Clinical procedures are performed by the appropriate licensed professional.
03

Mock-ups & provisionals

Plan the desired result before the definitive restoration is made.

Diagnostic and full-contour mock-ups

A diagnostic mock-up is a preliminary representation of a proposed treatment outcome. It may be made on a cast, digitally or, when indicated, represented in the mouth by the clinician. A full-contour wax-up depicts the intended complete external shape of the restoration, including visible and functional surfaces.

  • Purpose: visualize form, proportions, alignment and proposed occlusion.
  • Communication: helps the clinician, technician and patient discuss the proposed outcome.
  • Planning: helps assess available space and guides subsequent preparation, provisionalization and restoration design.
  • Limitation: a mock-up is a proposal; fit and function still need verification and may require modification.
Records & casts / scans→Analyse form, space & occlusion→Diagnostic wax-up / digital design→Review planned form→Guide or index→Provisional / definitive planning

Provisional restorations

A provisional restoration is an interim restoration placed while the definitive restoration is being made or treatment is being evaluated.

Main functions

  • Protect prepared teeth and support patient comfort.
  • Maintain position, contacts and occlusal relationships.
  • Support aesthetics and function during treatment.
  • Allow the team to evaluate proposed contours and function.

Desired qualities

  • Appropriate fit and margins; suitable contours and contacts.
  • Acceptable strength, aesthetics and cleanability.
  • Appropriate occlusion and a smooth, polished surface.

General fabrication sequence

Assess case & records→Make guide / index where applicable→Form interim material→Trim & check fit→Check contacts & occlusion→Finish & polish

The clinician performs intraoral fitting and placement. The specific material and technique depend on whether the provisional is made directly or indirectly.

Exam distinctionMock-up: previews and communicates a proposed result. Provisional: an interim restoration that protects and functions during treatment. A mock-up or its index can help guide a provisional, but the terms are not interchangeable.
04

Metal-ceramic substructures

The metal framework supports the porcelain and contributes to fit and strength.

Definition

A metal-ceramic substructure, or metal coping/framework, is the metal foundation of a metal-ceramic restoration. Porcelain is applied over the appropriately prepared metal to produce the required contour and appearance.

Principles of design

  1. Accurate fit: the coping should fit the prepared die and allow appropriate marginal adaptation.
  2. Support for porcelain: create a shaped framework that supports porcelain rather than leaving long, unsupported ceramic areas.
  3. Space for ceramic: provide sufficient, reasonably uniform veneering space without unnecessarily weakening the framework.
  4. Smooth transitions: avoid sharp angles and abrupt changes that may concentrate stress or hinder ceramic application.
  5. Functional form: account for occlusion, connectors where relevant, and the contours planned in the wax-up.
  6. Compatible materials: select an alloy and ceramic system intended to work together and follow the manufacturer's directions.
Examine die & prescribed design→Plan full final contour→Reduce / design supported coping→Pattern and cast→Check fit & finish metal→Prepare for ceramic application
RememberFramework and veneering thicknesses are material- and case-dependent. Use the specific alloy and ceramic manufacturer's instructions; do not invent a universal numerical thickness in an exam answer.

Why ceramic compatibility matters

Metal and porcelain need suitable bonding and compatible thermal behaviour. A mismatch in their thermal expansion behaviour or poor framework design can contribute to stresses and ceramic failure. Compatibility and processing should follow the selected system.

05

Spruing, investing & casting

The lost-wax sequence is a high-value flowchart for the exam.

The complete sequence

Prepare die→Wax pattern→Attach sprue→Invest→Set investment→Burnout→Melt & cast alloy→Cool appropriately→Divest→Remove sprue & finish→Inspect fit
StageWhat happens and whyExam / quality point
Wax patternThe intended metal form is built on the die.Correct form, smooth surfaces and accurate margins help determine the quality of the casting.
SpruingA wax sprue is attached to the pattern, creating a channel for molten alloy to enter the mould after burnout.Proper location and size support metal flow and solidification; poor design can contribute to incomplete castings or porosity.
InvestingThe pattern and sprue are surrounded by a suitable refractory investment that sets around them.Correct material selection and mixing help form a mould able to withstand the casting process.
BurnoutThe invested ring is heated according to the investment system; the pattern material is removed and a mould space remains.Follow the material-specific heating schedule and allow adequate burnout.
CastingThe alloy is melted and introduced into the heated mould using the appropriate casting equipment.Use the correct alloy and casting procedure; avoid contamination and inappropriate overheating.
Cooling and divestingThe mould is cooled according to material instructions; the investment is removed to retrieve the casting.Cooling practices vary with the alloy and investment. Wear appropriate protective equipment.
Finishing and inspectionThe sprue is removed; the casting is cleaned, finished and checked on the die.Check margins, internal fit, contour and visible defects before the next stage.

Common defects: causes to investigate

Incomplete casting

Possible contributors include inadequate metal flow, inappropriate mould or alloy temperature, poor sprue design or insufficient casting force. Investigate the entire process; one defect can have several causes.

Porosity

Possible contributors include solidification shrinkage, gas entrapment, contamination or unsuitable spruing. Assess the defect location and processing history.

Rough surface

Consider pattern quality, investment mixing, bubbles, burnout and contamination as possible contributors.

Inaccurate fit

Consider pattern distortion, die preparation, investment handling and expansion, casting or finishing errors.

Memory aid — not a substitute for the full answerPattern → channel → mould → burnout → metal → inspect. Expand each word into the proper stage and its purpose when writing your answer.
06

Computer systems in a digital lab

This section introduces the Day 1 digital basics; detailed scanning and manufacturing come later.

Core system components

ComponentRole in a dental digital workflow
CPUExecutes instructions and performs general processing.
RAMTemporary working memory for active applications and files; insufficient memory can slow large designs.
GPUProcesses graphics; relevant when viewing and manipulating 3D models, subject to software requirements.
StorageStores the operating system, software, scans, designs and backups. An SSD can improve file and program loading.
Monitor and input devicesAllow the technician to view and manipulate designs. Screen settings affect appearance; a screen alone does not guarantee accurate shade matching.
ConnectivityUSB, network or other supported connections link workstations with scanners and other equipment.
Power and backupReliable power, access control and backups support continuity and help protect case data.

Operating systems and applications

Microsoft Windows and Apple platforms are environments on which compatible applications may run. A dental lab may use them for case records, communication, office documents and, where supported, scanning or CAD software. Not every dental application supports every platform: check the vendor's operating-system, hardware, device and licensing requirements before installation.

A simple digital case pathway

Receive prescription & records→Scan / import data→Review and design→Save & verify case file→Transfer securely for manufacture→Archive / back up
Digital good practiceConfirm patient and case identifiers, use secure access and approved transfer methods, keep backups, and check that the file and software are compatible. Follow local privacy rules and laboratory policies.
07

Short-answer bank

Read, cover the answer, then reproduce it in your own words.

1. Define tooth contour and state its importance.

Tooth contour is the shape and curvature of a tooth's crown surfaces. Appropriate contour supports natural appearance, contact with adjacent teeth, occlusal function and cleaning around the gingiva. Over- or under-contouring can compromise these relationships.

2. Differentiate functional and morphological occlusion.

Morphological occlusion concerns the form and arrangement of teeth and occlusal surfaces, including cusps, grooves and contact relationships. Functional occlusion concerns how the teeth contact during closure and mandibular movements, including guidance and interferences.

3. What is a diagnostic mock-up? Give two uses.

A diagnostic mock-up is a preliminary representation of a proposed treatment result. It helps assess the planned tooth form and occlusion and improves communication among the clinician, technician and patient. It can also guide provisional restoration planning.

4. State four functions of a provisional restoration.

It protects the prepared tooth, maintains tooth position and contacts, provides interim function and aesthetics, and helps evaluate the proposed contours and occlusion during treatment.

5. What is the role of a metal-ceramic substructure?

It is the metal foundation of a metal-ceramic restoration. It provides a fitted framework and supports the veneering porcelain. Its design must allow appropriate ceramic space, smooth contours and adequate support while using compatible materials.

6. Define spruing and investing.

Spruing is the attachment of a channel to a pattern so that molten alloy can enter the mould during casting. Investing is surrounding the pattern and sprue with a suitable refractory material to create a mould after the pattern is removed by burnout.

7. Write the steps of lost-wax casting.

Prepare the die → make the wax pattern → attach the sprue → invest and allow to set → burn out the pattern → melt and cast the alloy → cool according to instructions → divest → remove the sprue, finish and inspect the casting.

8. What is occlusal equilibration?

It is the planned modification of selected occlusal contacts to achieve an intended occlusal relationship. It follows clinical diagnosis, contact assessment and a treatment plan; the clinician checks and adjusts as indicated, then rechecks and polishes.

9. Name four components of a digital dental workstation and their roles.

The CPU processes instructions; RAM provides working memory; the GPU supports graphics and 3D visualization; and storage retains software and case files. The required specifications depend on the dental software and equipment.

10. Why should a dental laboratory check operating-system compatibility?

Dental scanners and CAD applications have specific operating-system, hardware, driver and licensing requirements. Compatibility checks help ensure the application and equipment work together reliably and protect the continuity of the workflow.

Exam techniqueFor a 2-mark answer, give the definition and the most relevant points. For a longer question, add a labelled flowchart, examples and precautions as appropriate. Marks and expected depth vary by institution.
