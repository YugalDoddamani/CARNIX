01

Dental ceramic basics

What the material is, what it does and why it must be handled carefully.

Definition

Dental ceramics are inorganic, non-metallic materials used to make or veneer dental restorations. Different systems have different compositions and manufacturing processes; their behaviour is not identical.

Material families in this day's topics

Material / restorationBasic ideaImportant distinction
Feldspathic porcelainA glass-rich porcelain valued for optical qualities; commonly used as a veneering material in compatible systems.Can produce highly aesthetic layering, but unsupported thin porcelain is susceptible to fracture.
Porcelain fused to metal (PFM)A metal framework is veneered with a compatible dental porcelain.Combines metal support with a tooth-coloured veneer. The framework must support the porcelain.
Zirconia-based restorationA ceramic framework or monolithic restoration made from a suitable zirconia product.Can be monolithic or veneered; zirconia materials and translucency vary by product.
Full-metal crownA crown made entirely of a dental alloy.Not a ceramic restoration. It is included here for comparison in the fixed-restoration syllabus.

Useful properties to write in exams

  • Aesthetics: optical qualities such as translucency and colour can imitate tooth appearance.
  • Brittleness: many dental ceramics are susceptible to crack initiation and fracture, especially under tensile stress or when poorly supported.
  • Wear behaviour: depends on the material and surface condition; a smooth, properly finished surface is important.
  • Chemical stability and biocompatibility: important reasons ceramics are used in the mouth, though behaviour depends on the product.
  • Processing: shrinkage, firing and finishing vary by system; inaccurate handling can affect fit and surface quality.
RememberGood aesthetics do not remove the need for appropriate support, material selection, occlusal design and careful finishing.
02

Feldspathic porcelain & sintering

Understand the sequence, then reproduce it as a flowchart.

What is sintering?

Sintering is a heat-driven process in which particles consolidate into a more coherent mass. In conventional feldspathic dental porcelain firing, a glassy phase softens and flows to help the particles fuse and reduce porosity. The result and firing cycle depend on the product and furnace.

General porcelain build-up and firing

Prepare compatible support→Mix porcelain powder & liquid→Apply & shape layers→Condense / remove excess liquid→Dry & fire per system→Cool as directed→Inspect & add / refire if needed→Contour & finish

Why do condensation, firing and cooling matter?

  • Condensation: helps pack particles and remove excess liquid; poor handling can leave defects or distort the intended shape.
  • Firing: bonds and consolidates the ceramic; incorrect firing can affect surface, strength, colour and dimensions.
  • Shrinkage: built-up porcelain can shrink during firing, so contours may require correction and additional firing.
  • Cooling: follow the prescribed cycle to reduce unwanted thermal stresses; do not assume one cooling rate fits every system.
Common confusionDo not treat firing schedules or temperatures as universal. Feldspathic veneer firing and the sintering of a zirconia framework are different material-specific processes. Follow the correct instructions for each.
03

Layering, contouring & finishing

A restoration is not finished when it leaves the furnace.

Layering for a natural result

Layering combines porcelain masses with different optical properties to reproduce depth and natural tooth appearance. In a typical PFM system, an opaque layer masks the metal; body/dentine porcelain provides the main colour; and enamel/incisal porcelain adds translucency. Specific layers and firing sequences vary by system.

Framework prepared→Opaque layer for metal, when indicated→Body / dentine build-up→Enamel / incisal build-up→Fire & inspect→Correct contour / contact→Finish, stain or glaze as prescribed

Finishing terms

TermMeaningWhat to check
ContouringShaping the restoration to the intended tooth form after processing.Axial form, cusp anatomy, embrasures, proximal contacts and occlusion on the casts.
StainingApplying compatible colourants to modify or characterize appearance.Use restrained, prescribed characterization; a stain does not replace correct underlying shade and form.
GlazingProducing a glazed surface by a suitable firing process, with or without added glaze material as the system specifies.Follow instructions; inspect for a uniform, appropriate surface.
PolishingMechanically smoothing and finishing the surface using suitable instruments and sequences.A smooth finish is especially important after adjustments. Use the material-specific polishing system.
Practical safetyUse appropriate extraction and personal protection when grinding or polishing. Avoid excessive pressure and heat; inspect for surface defects. Final intraoral contact and occlusion assessment belongs to the clinician.
04

Colour science & shade matching

Separate the three colour dimensions before discussing translucency and texture.

TermSimple meaningExam example
HueThe basic colour family.Whether a tooth appears more yellowish or reddish.
ChromaThe intensity or saturation of the hue.A stronger or weaker intensity of the same colour family.
ValueThe relative lightness or darkness.A higher-value tooth looks lighter; a lower-value tooth looks darker.
TranslucencyThe extent to which light passes through and scatters within material.Incisal areas may appear more translucent than the body.
MetamerismTwo colours match under one light source but appear different under another.A match under clinic lighting may differ under daylight.

Shade communication workflow

Clean teeth & arrange suitable lighting→Assess shade before dehydration where possible→Compare shade tabs briefly→Record value, hue, chroma & characteristics→Communicate shade map / photographs→Reassess restoration with clinician

Record the shade guide and tab used, along with cervical, body and incisal differences, translucency, surface texture and any relevant photographs. Lighting, background, tooth hydration and viewer fatigue can affect perception. Communication should follow the clinic's and laboratory's procedures.

Exam distinctionShade matching is choosing and communicating the desired appearance. Staining is one method of modifying or characterizing the restoration's appearance; it is only one part of the overall process.
05

Fixed crowns & bridges

Learn the vocabulary before comparing manufacturing workflows.

Key definitions

  • Crown: a restoration that covers a prepared tooth to restore its form and function.
  • Coping: a fitted substructure that forms the foundation for a veneered restoration.
  • Fixed partial denture (FPD) / fixed dental prosthesis: a fixed restoration used to replace one or more missing teeth, supported by prepared teeth and/or implants according to the case.
  • Abutment: the supporting tooth or implant component for a prosthesis.
  • Pontic: the artificial tooth replacing a missing natural tooth.
  • Connector: the part joining units of a fixed prosthesis, such as a pontic to a retainer.

Compare the three restoration types

FeatureFull-metal crownPFM crownZirconia crown
StructureDental alloy throughout.Metal coping plus compatible porcelain veneer.Zirconia-based; monolithic or framework plus veneer.
AppearanceMetallic.Tooth-coloured veneer masks the metal, but metal may affect optical appearance.Tooth-coloured; optical appearance varies by material and design.
Workflow focusPattern, cast or manufacture, fit, finish and polish.Metal framework fabrication followed by porcelain build-up and firing.Digital design, manufacturing and zirconia sintering as indicated; finish or veneer according to the system.
Key checksMargins, fit, contacts, occlusion and surface finish.Those same checks plus framework support and porcelain integrity.Those same checks plus material-specific thickness, processing and surface finish.

Multiple-unit restorations & bridges

For multiple units, the technician considers the intended path of insertion, supporting structures, individual margins, connector design, pontic form and cleanability, contacts and occlusion. Connector dimensions and indications are case- and material-specific.

Review prescription & records→Assess abutments, space & occlusion→Design retainers, pontic & connectors→Fabricate framework / full contour→Check fit & finish→Clinical verification
06

Two fabrication workflows

These are general teaching sequences, not universal product instructions.

A. Porcelain fused to metal: PFM

Receive case & shade→Prepare die / design coping→Make metal framework→Check fit & prepare surface→Apply opaque & fire as directed→Build dentine / enamel porcelain→Fire, inspect & correct→Contour, shade-check & finish

Why does porcelain bond to the metal? Bonding involves interactions at the metal–oxide–porcelain interface, along with other contributing mechanisms such as mechanical retention and compatible thermal behaviour. The alloy and porcelain must be compatible; surface preparation and oxidation procedures depend on the system.

B. Zirconia: distinguish framework from veneer

Scan & design→Mill / manufacture zirconia→Sinter as specified for the material→Check fit & contour→Monolithic: finish / stain / glaze as prescribedORVeneered: apply compatible porcelain & fire→Inspect final restoration

Use the manufacturer's sequence for the chosen zirconia product. Veneering porcelain needs adequate zirconia support and compatible processing; an unsupported veneer is more vulnerable to chipping.

Quality check for both workflowsBefore delivery, assess fit and margins, proximal contacts, occlusion on the available records, contour, shade, cleanliness and surface integrity. The clinician makes the final intraoral assessment.

Defects and possible contributing factors

Chipping or cracking

Possible contributors include poor framework support, unsuitable thickness or connector design, processing or cooling errors, and excessive loading. Investigate the case rather than assigning one universal cause.

Shade or surface mismatch

Possible contributors include inadequate shade communication, unsuitable layer thickness, firing changes, staining, texture or different lighting.

07

Intraoral & extraoral scanning

Both create digital models, but they capture different objects.

Definitions

An intraoral scanner captures surfaces directly in the patient's mouth. An extraoral laboratory scanner captures an object outside the mouth, such as a dental cast, die or impression, depending on scanner capability.

FeatureIntraoralExtraoral / laboratory
Scanned objectTeeth and accessible oral surfaces directly.Physical cast, die or impression compatible with the device.
WorkflowClinician captures the mouth digitally; data can be sent to the laboratory.Lab digitizes a physical record before digital design.
ConsiderationsAccess, moisture control, scan strategy, patient movement and visible margins.Quality of the physical record/cast, positioning, calibration and scanner access to surfaces.
Shared goalCapture enough reliable data for design, fit and intended manufacturing; verify completeness and case identifiers.

General scan-to-design flowchart

Check prescription & identify case→Prepare scanner / follow infection control→Capture prepared area & surrounding surfaces→Capture opposing arch & bite record as needed→Inspect for missing or distorted areas→Rescan if needed→Send / import into CAD

What makes a scan useful?

  • Complete recording of the prepared area and margins as visible, adjacent contacts and sufficient surrounding anatomy.
  • Accurate relationship to the opposing arch when required for the case.
  • No obvious gaps, duplicated surfaces or distortions; appropriate rescanning where necessary.
  • Correct patient/case identification, supported file format and secure transfer.

Scanning and software capabilities differ. Follow device instructions, hygiene policies and the clinician's prescription.

08

Exam-ready short answers

Practise: definition first, followed by 3–4 relevant points.

1. Define dental ceramic. State two properties.

Dental ceramics are inorganic, non-metallic materials used for dental restorations or veneers. They can offer tooth-like optical properties and chemical stability, but many are brittle and require appropriate support and handling.

2. What is sintering in dental porcelain?

Sintering is heat-driven consolidation of particles into a coherent mass. In feldspathic porcelain firing, the glassy phase softens and helps particles fuse and porosity decrease. The firing cycle depends on the material system.

3. Give the functions of opaque, dentine and enamel porcelain in PFM.

Opaque porcelain helps mask the metal framework. Body or dentine porcelain contributes much of the main tooth colour and form. Enamel or incisal porcelain helps reproduce translucency and appearance near the incisal area.

4. Differentiate staining, glazing and polishing.

Staining modifies or characterizes colour using compatible colourants. Glazing creates a glazed surface through the appropriate firing process. Polishing mechanically smooths and finishes the restoration using suitable instruments.

5. Define hue, chroma and value.

Hue is the basic colour family; chroma is the intensity or saturation of that colour; value is its lightness or darkness.

6. What is a PFM crown?

A porcelain-fused-to-metal crown consists of a fitted metal substructure veneered with compatible porcelain. The metal provides support, while the porcelain contributes a tooth-coloured surface. Fit, framework design and compatible processing are important.

7. Differentiate a pontic from an abutment.

A pontic is the artificial tooth that replaces a missing tooth in a fixed prosthesis. An abutment is the supporting tooth or implant component for the prosthesis. A connector joins units of the prosthesis.

8. Compare intraoral and extraoral scanners.

An intraoral scanner captures surfaces directly in the patient's mouth. An extraoral laboratory scanner digitizes an object outside the mouth, such as a cast or impression. Both produce digital data that can be used for design, provided the scan is complete and accurate enough for the case.

9. Write the basic PFM fabrication sequence.

Review prescription and shade → design and fabricate the metal framework → check fit and prepare the surface → apply opaque porcelain → build dentine and enamel layers → fire and inspect → contour and finish → verify the final restoration.

10. What should be checked in a finished crown?

Check fit and margins, proximal contacts, occlusal relationships on available records, contour, shade and surface finish, as well as visible cracks or other defects. The clinician performs the final intraoral assessment.
